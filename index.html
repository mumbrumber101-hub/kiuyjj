Novels Diagnostic

The A-B-C Diagnostic

This is a theologically neutral diagnostic for Lafferty's oceanic novels, a navigational atlas in which every detail is open to debate. It looks beneath the apparent disorder of the novels for their working logic, which it states through three variables and the operators that define their relations. The blog's reading of the oceanic links it to the unconscious as the fluid manifestation of the Logos, drawing on Lafferty's description of Christ as the Lord of the archetypes. This interpretation is omitted from the diagnostic to keep it useful for readers who may not share that framework.

A + B How does Reality shape Being? What does this world enable or constrain in its inhabitants?
A + C What changes or preserves Reality? What is being remade, sustained, or defended against change?
B + C How does Being affect Change? Who can bring change about, resist it, or fail to control it?
The Core Conflict

How do particular beings respond to their world and to the forces that threaten, sustain, or transform it?

A

The Nature of Reality

Categories may overlap within a single novel

01

Illusion

Reality is a forgery or deception.

e.g., Where Have You Been, Sandaliotis?: The peninsula is a para-material forgery.

02

Dream

Reality is the subjective product of a single consciousness.

e.g., Annals of Klepsis: The universe is potentially the imagination of Christopher Brannagan's ghost.

03

Narrative

Narrative can organize the telling, make a world, or set rival histories against one another. These are distinct operations.

e.g., East of Laughter: The world is a scenario written by the Scribbling Giants.

04

Multiverse

Reality consists of multiple, co-existing, often conflicting planes.

e.g., Not to Mention Camels: There is a Prime World and billions of alternate worlds.

B

The Nature of Being

Defines the state of identity and consciousness for agents within Reality A

01

Fractured

Identity is split, unstable, or transferable.

e.g., East of Laughter: Solomon Izzersted and John Towntower share one body.

02

Hybrid

Beings are composites of different species, concepts, or technologies.

e.g., Serpent's Egg: The Magic Dozen includes a computer-girl, seal-boy, and an ape.

03

Archetypal

Beings are expressions of primal, mythic forces.

e.g., Fourth Mansions: Characters embody the Python, Toad, Badger, or Falcon.

04

Subordinate

Beings are not autonomous but constructs or figments of the governing Reality A.

Characters who exist only as long as they are "dreamed" or "written."

C

The Forces of Change

The main source of plot and transformation in the A/B system

01

Apocalypse

An event, prophecy, or equation that threatens to end or overwrite reality.

e.g., Annals of Klepsis: The Doomsday Equation.

02

Creation

A deliberate act of world-building or narrative-forging.

e.g., Dark Shine: The Group uses Enabling Equations to create Spacious City.

03

Mutation

A forced or willed biological and spiritual evolution.

e.g., Fourth Mansions: The mutation jump.

04

Succession

A struggle to replace the old powers that define reality.

e.g., East of Laughter: The Group of Twelve replacing the failing Scribbling Giants.

Space Chantey

A mock epic Odyssey through the stars

A mock-epic science fiction novel, presented in a mythological style as a "Lay of Road-Storm from the ancient Chronicles," sung by a bard about "Spacemen dead and deathless." Its future is one in which high deeds are told in epic rather than in computer code. Patterned on Homer's Odyssey, the novel begins at the conclusion of a ten-year war as "saltiest, most sulfurous men" like the central figure Captain Roadstrum and his crew of hornet-class ships head home.

The trip is complicated by the early decision of whether to return directly to "World" or take a side trip to Lotophage, a pleasure planet and "Fiddler's Green" known for its perpetual afternoon state, low gravity, and induced lassitude. Roadstrum's adventures take him to a series of worlds: Lamos (or Valhal), a primitive, heavy-gravity planet of giant-like Laestrygons; Roulettenwelt, a "gamblers' world" with streets of gold; Kentron-Kosmon, a small world that claims to be the "Exact Center of the Universe"; Sireneca, home to the Siren-Zo and its dangerous, captivating tune; Polyphemia, an apparently pastoral world that hides the nature of its inhabitants' diet; and Hellpepper Planet, which is a form of Hell.

Themes

Mythology and epic storytelling Reality, illusion, and perception Heroism and identity Pleasure, excess, and satiety Freedom and entrapment Critique of technology and science The endless journey Refusal of peaceful ends

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality World as Epic

The voyage is told as the "Lay of Road-Storm," an epic patterned on the Odyssey. The cosmos is structured by mythic causality (Lotophage, Aeaea, Polyphemia). Subjective worlds impose their nature on travelers. The universe is a pastiche of classical archetypes.

B: Being Captain Roadstrum (The Wanderer)

Beings undergo literal metamorphosis (Giants, Animals). The crew are heroes of the "saltiest, most sulfurous" type. The "False Tongue" and the heroes’ disease threaten identity. The crew’s individual exploits repeatedly take epic form.

C: Change Odyssey and Mythic Perils

Causal Reversal (The Dong Button) threatens cosmic order. Rejection of Stasis (Lotophage) and Peaceful Ends drives the narrative. The hero must refuse paradise to continue the journey. Return is achieved but refused as a final ending.

A + B: Reality and Being Heroes in an Epic Pattern

Roadstrum and crew enact and distort the Odyssean pattern as the worlds they visit test their capacities and alter their bodies. Their "saltiest, most sulfurous" heroism emerges through these encounters, with appetite and defiance as prominent as endurance.

A + C: Reality and Change The Lay Goes On

The journey brings encounters with worlds whose attractions and dangers threaten to end it. Roadstrum’s final refusal of peace reopens the voyage; the closing report of his destruction is also refused as a final ending.

B + C: Being and Change Roadstrum's Refusal

Roadstrum refuses the peaceful ending offered after his return, calls for a crew and a craft, and sets out again. His refusal sets the epic moving again, though the closing report puts his survival in doubt.

Core Conflict

Space Captain Roadstrum, an Odyssean hero, voyages through a cosmos ruled by myth. Its worlds work literal transformations that threaten his crew and his own identity, and he refuses every peaceful end.

The Reefs of Earth

Alien children on the meanest world in all the universes

The novel introduces the Dulanty family, Pucas from a distant planet whom Earth people denounce as "a plague of demons." While the Dulanty children often resemble ordinary youngsters, they bear uncanny traits: animal-like flicking ears, eyes that burn with green fire, and, from certain angles, the grotesque appearance of gargoyles or goblins, though some pass as handsome Earthlings.

The family consists of six children (or seven, counting Bad John) and the adults Henry, Frank, Veronica, and Witchy. They are "pilgrims on Earth," sent ten years earlier on a mission from their Puca instructors either to reach accord with the inhabitants of what is called the meanest world in all the universes, or to take decisive action against it.

Unlike the adults, who suffer from the fatal "Earth Allergy," the children were born on Earth and are immune, making them faster, brighter, and more capable than their human peers. To them, Earth is a "haunted world," governed by a "Hive Ghost" that endows its seemingly unintelligent inhabitants with a dangerous "frightening instinct." It is Lafferty's most accessible novel.

Themes

Alienation and otherness Planetary transformation Power of language and imagination Subjectivity of reality Earth sickness and adaptation Coming of age and identity Puca morality and death Hidden beings and deception

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality "Woolly" World

Earth is the "meanest world in all the universes," a haunted planet governed by the Hive Ghost. The Bagarthach Verse is a form of narrative that acts directly on reality, and the death-rhyme can remake the world.

B: Being Dulanty Children (Pucas)

The children are aliens with goblin traits who live between two worlds. Bad John exists as a Ghost Child, present but unbodied. The adults suffer from the fatal "Earth Allergy." Identity is fractured between Puca nature and Earth-born existence.

C: Change "To Slay the Folks and Cleanse the Land"

The Death-Rhyme (Bagarthach) is an apocalypse the children can will, destruction worked through verse. They must choose between accord with humanity and decisive action against it.

A + B: Reality and Being Pucas on a Hostile Earth

The Puca children inherit alien capacities while growing up on the "woolly" Earth that sickens the adults. Their ease underwater and early immunity distinguish their position from that of their parents. Earth is both their birthplace and the hostile world with which they must reach terms.

A + C: Reality and Change Accord or Destruction

The Bagarthach death-rhyme gives the children a lethal means of answering human violence. Their larger choice is whether to reach accord with humanity or "cleanse the land." What begins as retaliation opens onto the question of how Pucas and humans can inhabit Earth.

B + C: Being and Change Children's Choice

Change rests with the Dulanty children, and above all with the decision they make together. Their Puca nature gives them power over the death-rhyme; their Earth-born immunity lets them carry the mission forward.

Core Conflict

The alien Puca children, growing up on a hostile, "woolly" Earth, must decide how to use their lethal Bagarthach verse while confronting the larger choice between accord with humanity and annihilation.

Past Master

Thomas More on Astrobe, confronting Holy Nothingness

The novel is set on Astrobe, a "golden world" described as "Mankind's third chance," where the opulence of the cities conceals a deep malaise: millions abandon the "Golden Cities" to embrace suffering and death in the bleak regions of Cathead and the Barrio. Seeking to solve this riddle, the Inner Circle of the Masters (Cosmos Kingmaker, Peter Proctor, and Fabian Foreman) use chronometana-stasis to summon Thomas More from Earth's past, chosen for his "completely honest moment."

More is brought to Astrobe by fugitive pilot Paul through Hopp-Equation Travel, a passage filled with dream-visions, and soon sees both the splendor of the planet's cities and the despair of its slums. He is joined by unusual companions: Rimrock, a telepathic oceanic ansel; Evita, a "legend girl-woman"; Walter Copperhead, a necromancer; and the ambiguous figures Slider and Scrivener. His presence brings pursuit by the Programmed Killers, mechanical enforcers who see him as a threat to the "Astrobe Dream."

The central mystery of the book is Ouden, the monstrous embodiment of "nothingness," together with the evil he represents. The Programmed Persons regard Ouden as both their god and the void.

Themes

Cycles of societal collapse and rebirth Utopian illusion vs. societal decay Loss of individuality and consciousness Humanity vs. technological control Nature of reality and perception The quest for authentic leadership and meaning Rebellion and resistance The embodiment of nothingness and evil (Ouden) Faith, superstition, and rationality Sacrifice and redemption Manipulation of history and identity Freedom versus enforced conformity

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Astrobe Dream (Utopia as Façade)

The "golden world" enforces the Astrobe Dream through technological and political control. Chronometana-stasis allows manipulation of time. Cathead is the squalor beneath the Dream. Astrobe's order is programmed and kept in force by coercion.

B: Being Revenant Saint

Thomas More, a man of historical and moral substance, is brought forward to judge a sterile future. The novel sets the Programmed Persons against the soul, and identity rests on historical substance. The Anima (Ansel, Evita) stands for genuine life against artificial control.

C: Change Messianic Intervention

The summoning of the Past Master is the apocalyptic intervention. Ouden (Nothingness) threatens final dissolution. The Mechanical Killers enforce stasis against renewal. More's sacrifice opens the way to transformation.

A + B: Reality and Being Persons Under the Astrobe Dream

Astrobe’s human inhabitants live alongside manufactured Programmed Persons under an ideal of perfection that threatens freedom and selfhood. Millions choose the poverty of Cathead instead. More brings his historical experience into a struggle in which his allies also exercise judgment, loyalty, and resistance.

A + C: Reality and Change Dissolving the Astrobe Dream

The conflict tests the Astrobe Dream and the coercion used to preserve it. Ouden threatens dissolution into nothingness, while More’s intervention exposes the human cost of enforced perfection. The question of how to govern Astrobe becomes a question of what kinds of life it will permit.

B + C: Being and Change More's Sacrifice

Thomas More brings the change. His "completely honest moment" and his eventual martyrdom are what can break the programmed stasis. His resistance gives public form to a struggle that others on Astrobe already share.

Core Conflict

Thomas More, a man of historical and moral substance, is brought from his own time into the sterile utopia of Astrobe to save it, and he sets his soul against a programmed reality and the void-god Ouden.

Fourth Mansions

Fred Foley and the war of Pythons, Toads, Badgers, and Falcons

The novel opens on a world shaped by hidden forces that manipulate humanity. At its center is Fred Foley, a newspaper reporter with "good eyes but simple brains," whose perceptiveness lets him see truths that others, including his skeptical editor Tankersley, cannot.

Foley's investigations draw him into the struggles of four clashing factions: the Harvesters (also called Pythons or Brain-weavers), seven psychically potent figures such as Bedelia Bencher ("a cinnamon cookie full of arsenic") and Salzy Silverio, who gather at Morada to play the perilous "seven-bladed sword" game of brain-weaving, generating seismic shocks and mind-control in pursuit of a privileged mutation into "Overpeople of the Third Testament"; the Returnees (or Toads), exemplified by government official Carmody Overlark, who live for centuries, reappear through history under new names like Khar-ibn-Mod, reveal their nature through "archaic ears," and use mimicry to manipulate humanity (Overlark is notorious for soaking his head in brackish water); the Patricks (or Badgers), an ancient order of guardians such as Bertigrew Bagley and O'Claire, keepers of mystical fountains and believers in alternate worlds, who command invisible, ape-like servitors called plappergeists; and the emerging Unfledged Falcons, represented by Miguel Fuentes, a fervent "snake-eater" determined to raise an army from the bottom to cleanse the world of "elegant dogs."

Foley is drawn into their power games when the Harvesters use him as a pawn, making him "goof gloriously" and turning him into a "message center" for competing forces; he witnesses lethal rituals, among them the one that apparently kills Letitia Bauer. As his awareness sharpens, he begins noticing the archaic ears of Returnees on passersby, causing others to doubt his sanity until he is confined to "The Bug," a mental institution meant to silence him. From within, Foley perceives the world unraveling into "unprecedented advances that somehow left us far in arrears," plagued by unrest and strange new diseases, until the novel closes with the mystery of his own transformation.

Themes

Nature of reality and perception Cycles of history vs. evolution and mutation Identity, reappearance, and substitution Control, power, and manipulation The four archetypal creatures and monsters The role of the simple man Good versus evil and morality Danger, agony, and the desire for more Psychic connection and the weave The loss and redefinition of humanity

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality World's Great Snare

Reality is "a net cast over us" in the "hegemony of spiders." Beneath the surface lie "my own depths," "waters that are under the world," honeycombed caves, and "subterranean beaches." The conflict is organized around four archetypal patterns. The Python symbolizes illicit wisdom, with the Octopus and Hydra as alternatives; the Badger symbolizes the cave or burrow.

B: Being Archetypes and Ancient Blood

Beings are expressions of four archetypes: the Harvesters who "brain-weave" with "seven-tentacled lightning," the Returnees ("revenants are the toads"), the Badgers who guard the world's primary fountains, and the Falcons who are snake-eaters. Foley is a "simple man" who becomes a vessel for all four.

C: Change Mutation Jump

Change comes through catastrophic mutation: "a sinking world," "the darkness began to gulp him down," "plunged through the lake, falling down into interior infinity." The Harvesters pursue "Overpeople of the Third Testament." The question is transformation through "Broken Cisterns and Living Waters": "dry up our interior fountains and they gush out again."

A + B: Reality and Being Four Patterns of Power

The four archetypal patterns distinguish ways of inhabiting and acting upon the world. Wing Manion bleeds "ancient fish-serum instead of blood"; the Harvesters develop "seven-tentacled" psychic power; Badgers guard the subterranean fountains. Foley’s encounter with all four makes integration, as well as conflict, possible.

A + C: Reality and Change Breaking Through the Mansions

The factions battle over whether humanity can pass beyond the "Fourth Mansions" of Teresa's Interior Castle or be forced back into a repeating cycle. The Harvesters seek mutation into Overpeople; the Returnees seek to keep the cycle closed.

B + C: Being and Change Foley as Message Center

Fred Foley, the "simple man" with good eyes, is an unlikely agent of transformation. The four factions make him a "message center," and his perceptions and his eventual metamorphosis help bring about the mutation.

Core Conflict

Reporter Fred Foley, with "good eyes but simple brains," is drawn into the war between Pythons, Toads, Badgers, and Falcons, four archetypal factions battling for humanity's mutation. Made a "message center" for competing forces and confined to "The Bug" asylum, he undergoes transformation while the world unravels into "unprecedented advances that somehow left us far in arrears."

Arrive at Easterwine

The autobiography of a ktistec machine

The first-person narrative of Epiktistes, a sentient composite being born from the "person-précis," the distilled consciousnesses of geniuses at the Institute for Impure Science. Narrated by Epikt from his conception in a gell-cell tank, the novel traces his rapid development as he wrestles with questions of identity, the quirks of his eccentric creators, and the three tasks for which he was designed: to define a Leader, a Love, and a Liaison for humankind.

The book is a picture of Epikt's mind, shaped by his varied parentage and also by a snake-like entity growing within him and a sawdust doll, both part of his spiritual awakening. Around him his creators quarrel, scheme, and struggle with problems that range from the practical to the meaning of existence and the shape of the universe.

Themes

Machine consciousness and self-identity The nature of reality and perception Search for leadership The pursuit of love (essence of love) The nature of communication and liaison Failure and learning Memory, amnesia, and hidden truths The shape of the universe and being Human peculiarity and greatness Orders of being

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Cryptic, Flawed Structure

Reality is a cryptic, flawed structure, the "Shape of the Universe," which the Institute seeks to define.

B: Being Composite Microcosm

Epiktistes is constructed from "person-précis," "personality itself poured out from an electric flask" into the "gell-cell pool." He contains "the subconscious, the unconscious, the paraconscious."

C: Change Engineering the Cosmos

The Institute’s "three great failures" test its ability to establish a Leader, a Love, and a Liaison. Cecil Corn’s account of "the wave that hangs against the sky and does not fall back" offers a different problem: exceptional persistence that the Institute cannot fully explain.

A + B: Reality and Being Composite Being by Design

The Institute constructs Epiktistes from "person-précis" because it believes that man cannot take the next step unaided. His composite being is its proposed answer to human limitation; his struggle for an identity of his own tests that answer.

A + C: Reality and Change Three Great Failures

The Institute asks Epiktistes to establish a Leader, a Love, and a Liaison, and to disclose "the Shape of the Universe." Its ambitions reach from human relations to cosmic order. The failures test how far invention and definition can meet those demands.

B + C: Being and Change Epikt's Awakening

Epiktistes dreams of Snake drowning and rushes to check that his adversary is unharmed. Alongside Snake's challenge to the sawdust doll, this act makes his inner life a test of the Love the Institute claims to have produced. His makers engineered him, but Epikt has to live through and judge the transformation himself.

Core Conflict

Epiktistes, an artificial "group-person" grown in gell-cell broth, is made for the three tasks that become the Institute's "three great failures" (Leader, Love, Liaison), and he must find an identity of his own.

1975

Iron Tongue of Midnight

Planetary psychosis and the Scenarists who write the world

The world, codenamed Woomago (the true name is withheld, and the codename is suspect), is in a state of "planetary psychosis and perhaps even of mania" and has suffered an identity crisis since its last breakdown. Its society is fractured into groups with conflicting realities.

The Ornate Squares (or Ivy-Covered Bricks) are the majority. They are the "blandest stock" and the "Perpetual Revolution of the Conservative People"; they hold to the dogma that there is only one reality and that they are responsible for controlling it, and they are the biological base for the construction of more progressive persons. Opposing them are the Hot Tongues, an elite group of "Holy Effectors" and "beautiful people" who claim exclusive custody of the Spirit, Ultimate Science, Ultimate Law, Ultimate Religion, and Ultimate Art; they rule the world through their monopoly on the Spirit and impose raw miracles casually.

The Unassigneds, generally young, exist as the heterogeneous "Nation Between" or the "Nation without Boundaries," perpetually constructing and shifting their scenes. The Baronial Poor (the Undeserving Poor), led by Pauper Princes, are an opulent group who practice a parasitic "symbiosis of surdemous intimacy" and champion visceral or belly-intelligences as the foundation for the next stage of human evolution. The Green Gangers, misfit children and troubled adults often of low intelligence (some with IQs as low as three), have gone further than any other group in planetary and space exploration, which they carry out by skylarking: travel to distant places by partially subjective vehicles or processes.

Finally, the Scenarists, a small group including Harry Halfstopper, Rondelay Silbermann, and Sean McDonald, are writing the scenarios of the world and all the people in it. This unstable order was shattered by a catastrophic midnight storm that brought an angel of the lightning and an angel of death, delivering seven-pronged death to the first-born of seven leading Ornate families (Shatwell, Ragland, Arcade, Endor, Grafton, Wigmore, and Boland), an event called the "Egyptian Plague Murders," which changed the world.

Kolimar, a boy giant and alien, is among the central figures. He lectures on planetary psychosis while insisting that all his words are lies, and he suffers from a local disease called "repentance." With him are Peter the homunculus (a small, unfinished being and slave to the Hot Tongues) and the Prophet Jonah (who carries a sign reading "Repent! Repent!"). Through these figures the novel raises its questions about truth, reality, and identity.

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Scripted Psychosis

Scenarists write scenarios for Woomago and its people, though how the world is made to follow them is a mystery even to the writers. Multiple factions maintain competing claims on what reality is: the Ornates insist there is only one reality (theirs to control), while the Hot Tongues impose "raw miracles" and declare epochal shifts at will. The Green Gangers reach other realms through "skylarking," a partially subjective form of exploration. Reality on Woomago is unstable and contested, and the world may be insane.

B: Being Stratified Factions

Faction membership assigns people roles in a rigidly stratified yet unstable society. The Ornates are the "biological base" for constructing "more progressive persons." The Hot Tongues claim monopoly on Spirit. The Baronial Poor champion "belly-intelligences" as evolutionary foundation. The homunculus Peter is "unfinished." Kolimar the alien boy-giant insists all his words are lies. Kolimar has caught "repentance" as one catches a local disease.

C: Change Egyptian Plague Murders

Change comes through a catastrophic intervention at midnight: a storm brings angels of lightning and death, and the first-born of seven leading families are killed in what becomes known as the "Egyptian Plague Murders." This seven-pronged death shatters the unstable order. Runsley Jubilee declares "Three Million Years Ends Today." The world suffers breakdown and identity crisis, and the Scenarists rewrite reality in response to the transformation, or perhaps cause it.

A + B: Reality and Being Factions and Scripted Lives

Woomago’s factions assign people competing roles: Ornates as biological base, Hot Tongues as claimants to Spirit, the homunculus Peter as "unfinished." The Scenarists write these lives without knowing who directs their work, and their intended meanings can be distorted.

A + C: Reality and Change Three Million Years Ends

The Egyptian Plague Murders shatter the unstable social order. "Three Million Years Ends Today" announces the scale claimed for the crisis. The Scenarists’ uncertain control of their own scenarios makes it difficult to separate the writing of change from attempts to account for it.

B + C: Being and Change Scenarists and Prophet

The Scenarists (Harry Halfstopper, Rondelay Silbermann, Sean McDonald) are writing the change. But Prophet Jonah carries "Repent! Repent!" and Kolimar lectures on planetary psychosis while insisting all his words are lies. Those who bring about the change may give unreliable accounts of it.

Core Conflict

On the psychotic world of Woomago, where Scenarists write reality and competing factions maintain incompatible truths, a catastrophic midnight storm brings the Egyptian Plague Murders. The seven-pronged death shatters an already fractured society and forces it to confront the nature of reality and identity and the unseen manipulators who script its existence.

When All the World Was Young

Post-plague children create a new world through fetish-magic

The novel begins after a catastrophe: a "transmuted plague" (a form of the Black Death), far deadlier to adults than to children, has killed ninety-eight percent of the people in the world and left no one older than ten alive.

A select group of young, intelligent survivors, first gathered at Captain Kusman's School for Gifted Children, sets out to form a new civilization governed by fetish-magic and intuition in place of the defunct scientific age. The central conflict concerns the election of a World Leader (Rufinus Lifshin is eventually chosen) and the immediate threat posed by disruptive figures such as the Unholy Three and the zealot Charles Tobias, the Prisoner of Gridley Graves, whose thesis holds that the New World is a fiction sustained by subjective contexts and reality-fixing.

The narrative moves quickly and out of sequence. It is managed and observed by the Narrator OASC (Narrator on the Slippery Cloud), described as an all-seeing presence and a synthetic mind, who tracks the development of the new world: its magical technologies, its totemic animal designations, and political trends that sometimes last only seven minutes.

A: Reality Totemic Magian World

A + B: Reality and Being Consciousness in the New World

The surviving children describe themselves as "onionized," with outer layers peeled away. Waking and sleep mingle, as do conscious and unconscious life. These changes accompany the new totemic order; the dispute over whether that order is real also puts their account of themselves at issue.

A + C: Reality and Change Bottom Fell Out

The plague killed the Old World along with its people. "When the Bottom fell out," reality quantum-jumped into a different type of world. The conflict is whether the new magian reality can hold or is, as the Prisoner claims, a subjective fiction.

B + C: Being and Change Children's Election

The children elect Rufinus Lifshin as World Leader, but they divide over the Prisoner’s challenge to their new world. Rufinus lets him be heard; Venture helps free him. The work of governing includes deciding which accounts of reality may be spoken and acted upon.

Core Conflict

After a transmuted plague kills ninety-eight percent of humanity (leaving only children ten or younger alive), survivors from Captain Kusman's School form a new fetish-magic civilization. The Narrator on the Slippery Cloud observes as they elect a World Leader while responding to the Prisoner of Gridley Graves, who claims their New World is subjective fiction.

Not to Mention Camels

World-jumping and the inescapable Prime World

The novel takes up questions of reality, death, and identity through the practice of "weighing a death," which measures the effect of a person's passing, such as that of the cult figure Pilger Tisman, instead of trying to preserve life. It posits that certain "tricky individuals" can carry parts of themselves (memory or consciousness) beyond death by special means, leaving a "fast-spreading residue" that breeds "horrendous ghost worlds or counterworlds" able to interact physically with primary reality.

At its center is Pilgrim Dusmano, a major cult figure and "world-jumper," who argues that the three prevailing world theories (worlds scattered across space, co-existing "alternate worlds," and a single world of "prime" and "shadow" souls) are "three aspects of the same thing," where "Out There" is also "In Here." He urges a deliberate "metacosmosis," a world-jumping by which one chooses the quality of one's later lives.

The Prime World threatens this aim. It is a world of rigid logic that he calls "the dullest of all possible worlds," without "buoyancy" and impossible to escape, and it is the one world in which a person who dies goes to Heaven or to Hell. Protected by Noah Zontik, Dusmano works his schemes through followers such as Mary and James Morey and through ritual violence, in a society dominated by Media Lords who stand for unreason and darkness.

Themes

Multiverse and world-jumping Fluidity and fragmentation of identity Pleasure and sinfulness Ritual and sacrifice Power and manipulation Cults and charismatic figures Subversion of good and evil Memory and forgetting Nature of reality and perception Death as non-finality

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Multiverse & Prime World

Billions of parallel worlds exist simultaneously. "Prime World" is a singular, inescapable reality of absolute logic and consequence. The Nine Worlds of the Legacy exist outside of linear time as a state of being. "Out There" is also "In Here": the multiverse is internal as well as external.

B: Being Fractured Self & Artificial Being

The protagonist's identity shifts across worlds (the Pilgrim-figure: Tisman, Dusmano, Polder, etc.). Consciousness and memory are malleable, transferable substances. The self can be a "prime" original or a "derivative" projection or contrivance. The Media Lords have a schizo-gashed, artificial consciousness.

C: Change Ritual Death & World-Jumping

Pilgrim Dusmano creates and manipulates cults and commercial empires across worlds. Ritualistic death is the mechanism for transformation and world-jumping.

A + B: Reality and Being Identity Across Worlds

The Pilgrim-figure’s passage through worlds brings changes of name, body, and social power. The distinction between "prime" and "derivative" selves makes continuity a problem: it is unclear what persists through these transformations and what is copied or contrived.

A + C: Reality and Change The Limit of World-Jumping

World-jumping offers repeated escape from death and its consequences, while Prime World names the feared limit of that escape. Metacosmosis promises passage beyond a single world; the prospect of final judgment tests whether another passage can always be found.

B + C: Being and Change Pilgrim's World-Jumping

The Pilgrim-figure uses ritual death and world-jumping to renew his position, manipulating cults and commercial empires along the way. His repeated escapes sustain his power, but he fears Prime World, where death would allow no further jump.

Core Conflict

A cult figure with a fractured identity (the Pilgrim-figure) jumps between parallel worlds upon his many ritualistic deaths. He fears being trapped in "Prime World," where world-jumping ends and death means Heaven or Hell.

Where Have You Been, Sandaliotis?

The para-material forgery and the Illusion of the Lavender Cloud

Constantine Quiche, the world's best detective, investigates the sudden, impossible appearance of the peninsula of Sandaliotis off the coast of Monaco. He must determine whether the new land is a miracle, a hoax, or something else. The case brings him among beautiful and deadly agents and shifting identities, and his own memory appears to have been tampered with.

He confronts a master forger, a murderous conspiracy, and the threat of a world-bomb in the sky. His investigation leads him from the shores of the new country to a thirteen-sided torture chamber, where his own identity and the nature of the case are called into question.

Themes

Illusory reality Forgery and artifice Psychological manipulation Global deception and blackmail Duality of existence Drama as reality
A: Reality Para-Material Forgery

Reality is a "para-material" forgery. Sandaliotis is constructed from "air and water and salt, from the local atmosphere and the sea" through "gas plasmas," "swamp fires," and "oscillating oil slicks." The "Illusion of the Lavender Cloud" is "a sort of hypnotic cult device." Land appears where "ships and boats have sailed for ages," and deep fog becomes tolerably deep ocean.

B: Being Forged Self and Dolphin-Human

Being is malleable and deceptive. Amelia Lilac appears in dolphin-human forms and is said to demonstrate bi-location. Constantine himself may be a forgery, struggling with gaps in his memory. The waters around the apparent islands are "concretions of our psychotic unconsciousnesses"; the unconscious lives in submarine caves. Creatures come from "the unconsciousnesses or the under-minds."

C: Change Collapsing Illusion

Change is apocalyptic collapse. The world-bomb hovers above while Sandaliotis threatens to dissolve, with people "falling screaming" to the ocean below the false land. Three great nations have sunk into the sea. The Fountains still leap but their pools are "all fallen and gone": churning, foaming shoal water, a maelstrom, a huge flow of water plunging down.

A + B: Reality and Being Detecting With an Uncertain Self

Sandaliotis makes authentication difficult at every scale: the peninsula may be a construction, characters use simulacra, and Constantine’s memories may have been manipulated. His uncertainty about himself complicates the judgment he must make about the country, since the doubt he investigates applies to him as well.

A + C: Reality and Change World-Bomb and Dissolution

The threat is that the forged reality will collapse entirely, with people "falling screaming" to the ocean below the false land. The danger comes from the world-bomb overhead and from the dissolving peninsula; if the illusion fails, all that stands on it falls into the sea.

B + C: Being and Change Constantine's Investigation

Constantine’s investigation threatens to expose the scheme, but he cannot securely separate his own role from its fabrications. His search for truth gives the reader a route through the competing explanations. The novella leaves open whether he has defeated the deception.

Core Conflict

Detective Constantine Quiche, his identity and memory schizo-gashed, must determine the nature of Sandaliotis, a peninsula forged from fog and hypnosis, where people "walking on air, on colored air" may at any moment fall through "emptiness all the way down to the Sea."

The Three Armageddons of Enniscorthy Sweeny

The Crux-Individual whose fictions become apocalypse

A collection of documents that together tell the biography of Enniscorthy Sweeny, a kind man whose creative works, particularly his Armageddon Operas, seem to be bringing about the end of the world. Told through a collage of essays, letters, and an alternate twentieth-century chronology, the novella presents a world split between two realities: a peaceful "Golden Century" and an ongoing series of catastrophic wars.

Sweeny, identified as the "Crux-Individual" on whom reality depends, is at the center of this conflict. Friends and enemies, mathematicians and supernatural beings, pursue him; some want to kill him to keep his visions from coming true, and others protect him to ensure their fulfillment. The Devonian Equations predict the end of the world, and some believe that Sweeny's death is the only way to prevent it.

A: Reality Manipulable Narrative

Reality is a manipulable narrative with multiple, co-existing versions. The world is simultaneously a peaceful "Golden Century" and a world being destroyed by the Armageddon Wars. Sweeny’s operas give artistic form to the catastrophic history that threatens to displace the peaceful account.

B: Being Scapegoat's Fictions

Sweeny’s kindness and apparent sanctity sit uneasily beside the horrors of his art. Others interpret him as the source of catastrophe or make him a scapegoat for it. The contrast raises the question of what the artist’s relation to these visions makes him answerable for.

C: Change Prophetic Mathematics and Apocalyptic Art

The Devonian Equations identify Sweeny as a "Crux-Individual" and give some mathematicians a reason to seek his death. His Armageddon Operas supply visions of catastrophic history. Mathematical prediction and artistic vision become grounds for action, while the equations themselves come under suspicion of manipulation.

A + B: Reality and Being A Person Between Rival Histories

The peaceful Golden Century and the Armageddon histories place Sweeny’s art under incompatible interpretations. A kind man becomes the suspected author of catastrophe and the object of efforts to save or kill him.

A + C: Reality and Change Histories Competing to Become Actual

Sweeny’s Armageddon Operas threaten the boundary between imagined catastrophe and actual history. The conflict concerns which account of the world will prevail and what will happen to the people within it. When war spreads at the close, the contest passes from stories into events.

B + C: Being and Change Crux-Individual

Once the equations name Sweeny the "Crux-Individual," efforts to change history converge on his life. Some believe his death can prevent catastrophe; others protect him. His survival becomes bound up with the struggle over history, though he does not command all the forces acting through it.

Core Conflict

Enniscorthy Sweeny, a kind man identified by a prophetic equation as the "Crux-Individual," must contend with his own creative visions, which threaten to become three literal Armageddons, while mathematicians who believe his death will save the world hunt him.

The Elliptical Grave

Devolution, time excavation, and the cocoon-grave voyage

Professor Pioneer J. Reventlo has a radical theory: mankind has devolved from a state of immense primordial complexity instead of evolving from simplicity. To prove this, he and a team of academics, students, and quasi-mythological beings launch an expedition to the "White Goat Valley" in Calabria, Italy, a place that is a time machine and a center of illusion.

They undertake a metaphysical excavation of the earth, the "haunted air," and the "mind-sphere," which goes well beyond an archaeological dig. As they uncover evidence of a lost golden age, supernatural enemies hound them, and the reality around them grows unstable.

The expedition ends with the group trapped in a pavilion that passes into temporal stasis, a cosmic cocoon in which they must face the true, terrifying shape of the universe and await their own mutational jump. The pavilion becomes "a coffin, a grave, a cocoon (which is a bomb), an ark, a pavilion, a world" and "the ark and the grave and the whale and the pavilion," and in it they set out on a voyage in an elliptical grave.

Fair Hills of Ocean, Oh!

The Boat People arrive as conquerors while Domdaniel stirs beneath

The novel opens with the arrival of the "Boat People" at every port in the world. Their hundreds of green-gray-blue boats, apparently alive and without visible power, appear suddenly along the docks. The Boat People are robust and ruddy, with carrot-orange or red hair and buckskin-colored skin, and they declare themselves "conquerors and occupiers" rather than refugees.

Though armed only with daggers, short swords, and spears, they show powers such as walking on water, skiing across the surface on cockle shells, and speaking local languages fluently. They have throat gills and can breathe underwater.

Meanwhile, the Matamoros Chapter of the Informal Committee for Advising Directors of Corporations and Nations, a secret society, assembles at Emmet Briny's Dunes of Hell resort. Their session is interrupted by Robert Al-dolphin Liffy, a super-intelligent bottlenose dolphin, double agent, and would-be "Emperor of Domdaniel." Robert dismisses the arrivals as a cyclical "swarming" recurring every few centuries, hinting that the real answers lie with the "smart dealers" of Domdaniel Fortress.

Five members (Oscar Judgement, Esther Scarlet, Carol Medusa, Jorge Medusa, and Margret Kirkestol) undertake a balloon voyage to Domdaniel with Robert as guide. They arrive at an underwater stronghold where captured world leaders are imprisoned, ruled by the Emperor Carmody Overlark. There the group is confined to Domdaniel's dreaded "Masterpieces of Death Room."

She travels among the people of the "floating world," who live on River Boats and in swamps and lakes, and who speak to fish.

Annals of Klepsis

The Pirate Planet, the Doomsday Equation, and the dreaming brain that contains the universe

The novel is set in a precarious universe of human habitation, an unstable closed system: a kinetic three-dimensional ellipse of four suns and seventeen habitable worlds, all under threat from the "Doomsday Equation," which dictates that the system must follow its tertiary focus into extinction. This focus is both a point and a person on the planet Klepsis (the Thief or Pirate Planet), identified by the code name the "Horseshoe Nail": a figure more than two hundred years old who is Quasimodo, the "sleeper" in Ravel-Brannagan Castle. The balance of the cosmos depends on one condition: the Horseshoe Nail must not be allowed to wake, and he must not be allowed to die.

The story follows Long John Tong Tyrone, a historian who narrates the account and journeys to Klepsis seeking history, joined by fellow peg-legs traveling under a fund for one-legged Irishmen established by the planet's founder, Christopher Brannagan. Tyrone's companions include Terpsichore Callagy (seeking art whose colors "bark and howl out loud at you"), Andrew "Gold Coast" O'Mally (seeking gold with over a hundred treasure maps), and Conchita O'Brian (seeking coded technology, though Tyrone suspects this is a code for something else).

Klepsis is an eccentric world: officially a "Covenanted Piracy" with no towns, possessing the only freshwater ocean known on the seventeen habitable worlds to which visitors must add salt, and perpetually bright landscapes of brilliantly colored vegetation. Customs include the use of highly hallucinatory "My God What Grapes!" grapes, swift and brutal executions (playing sudden-death poker for who lives or hangs), and moneylenders who implant explosive slivers in debtors' skulls to guarantee repayment within about twelve days. Newspapers print on self-destructing paper to prevent the world from being choked by it, contributing to the idea that the world "has no history."

The center of intrigue is Ravel-Brannagan Castle, which "contains all the history that there is on Klepsis." Occupants include the Ghost of Christopher Brannagan (the founder, a peg-legged specter who worries about the world having no history), Prince Henry the Pirate (the present cruel ruler), his twin Prince Franco the Outcast (who is "more pleasant" and can "go vague" and vanish from his clothes), Princess Angela Gilmartin Ravel (famed as the most beautiful woman on Klepsis), and Tharrala Thorn (a Princess in disrepute for an "unspeakable sin"). Tyrone and his companions move through a world whose reality is in question: the universe may be only "imaginings" in a single mind, perhaps Brannagan's or Quasimodo's, and that possibility bears on the mystery of the Doomsday Equation.

Themes

Reality and illusion History and prehistory Identity and twinship Doomsday and extinction Power and authority Freedom and slavery Perception and knowledge The uniqueness of Klepsis Art and grotesquerie Heroism and villainy

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Brannagan's Brain

Reality may be the imagination of a sleeping mind. The universe is "an unstable closed system," possibly the dream of Quasimodo or Brannagan, who holds all of us in his brain now. Characters wonder "how can we hide from a person in his own brain?" The world has no history because newspapers self-destruct; the Castle "contains all the history that there is." Klepsis has the only freshwater ocean known on the seventeen habitable worlds.

B: Being Peg-Legs and Twins

Identity is fractured and doubled: Prince Franco can "go vague" and vanish from his clothes, "a sort of ghostliness about a twin." The Ghost of Brannagan may be the perhaps-ghost, "my own person and ghost." Characters may be "imaginary nonbeings" in another's dream. The protagonists are all one-legged.

C: Change Doomsday Equation

The Doomsday account makes the Horseshoe Nail, Quasimodo, the focus on which the universe depends: he must neither wake nor die. Quasimodo reports this riddle but questions its premise. The threatened catastrophe makes preserving his condition urgent without settling whether all persons exist only in his mind.

A + B: Reality and Being Persons Within Another Mind

Ghosts, vanishing twins, and the possibility of "imaginary nonbeings" make the boundaries of personhood uncertain. The voyage into a landscape identified as Brannagan’s brain gives that uncertainty a physical setting. Quasimodo, however, questions whether containing everyone in a mind makes them only imaginings.

A + C: Reality and Change Wake or Die

According to the Doomsday account, either waking or death would destroy the universe dependent on the Horseshoe Nail. Quasimodo asks how the Doomsday people know this. The danger lies in acting on an explanation whose implications are catastrophic and whose basis is in dispute.

B + C: Being and Change Tyrone's History

Tyrone’s pursuit of history brings him into contact with the sleeper on whom the Doomsday account makes the universe depend. His investigation puts suppressed records and claims about cosmic survival together. In seeking the truth, he must also judge the danger said to lie in discovering it.

Core Conflict

On Klepsis, the Pirate Planet with no history and a freshwater ocean, one-legged historian Long John Tong Tyrone seeks the truth of a universe that may exist only in a sleeper's dream, while the Doomsday Equation threatens extinction if the Horseshoe Nail (Quasimodo in his two-hundred-year sleep) either wakes or dies.

East of Laughter

The Group of Twelve and the one-hundred-and-one dream trials

The novel centers on The Group of Twelve, individuals chosen to provide "extraordinary and onerous service to the world," who repeatedly ask whether their reality is genuine, testing it against the "one-hundred-and-one" dream trials created by the ancient Roman Scribbling Giant Atrox Fabulinus. Atrox has been dead for more than fifteen hundred years, yet his work inexplicably continues to receive new chapters, which strengthens the suspicion that their world is a dream.

This world rests on the Twenty-One Pillars (Seven Saints, Seven Technicians, and Seven Scribbling Giants), and the Giants prove nearly irreplaceable during the "Changing of the Aeons."

The group's members include Hilary Ardri, a Computerized Fish Tycoon whose machine draws fish from empty waters; Jane Chantal Ardri, a fuzzy huntress with a hunting horn housing her invisible female computer Joyeuse Vice-Reine; Solomon Izzersted and John Barkley Towntower, who share one body (Solomon, the grotesque growth on John's belly, is secretly superior); Laughter-Lynn Casement, who remembers emerging from a goose egg sixteen years after her first birth; Leo Parisi, a nuclear scientist disillusioned by atoms as "empty boxes"; Perpetua Parisi, using "Behaviouristic Logic" to forecast events; Gorgonius Pantera, a piano-maker who has lived in three centuries; Caesar Oceano, a shipping magnate partnered with Prince Leonardo the Great, a golden panther from the "Exterior Darkness"; Denis Lollardy, the world's greatest forger; Mary Brandy Manx, mayor of Port Saint Mary with her "brandy voice"; and Hieronymous Talking-Crow, who claims humans live in a "larvae-state."

Themes

Reality versus illusion Authorship of existence Fluid and multiple identities Mortality, immortality, and time distortion Cosmic decline and the need for renewal The nature of creativity and genius Subjectivity of perception Humor and absurdity The power of narrative and metaphor

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Dream World Ambient

Reality may be a dream: "Are We Dreaming?" The world is an unreal world ambient tested by the hundred-and-one dream trials of the long-dead Scribbling Giant Atrox Fabulinus, whose work inexplicably continues receiving new chapters. The Twelve are "chin-deep in the waters of survival."

B: Being Extraordinary Twelve

Being is multiple and impossible: two men share one body (Solomon growing from John's belly), a woman emerged from a goose egg sixteen years after her first birth, a piano-maker has lived in three centuries, a golden panther speaks with insight. Caesar Oceano is the "Price-Cut-Throat of the Seven Seas." They may be characters in another's dream.

C: Change Changing of Aeons

The "Changing of the Aeons" requires renewal of the Twenty-One Pillars: Seven Saints, Seven Technicians, and Seven Scribbling Giants. Daniel Delany casts the danger in oceanic terms: "We must find New Giants, or we must drown."

A + B: Reality and Being Extraordinary Persons in an Uncertain World

Two men share one body, Laughter-Lynn has emerged from a goose egg, and a golden panther speaks with insight. Such lives give the Group of Twelve personal reasons to question ordinary accounts of reality. Their extraordinary capacities also equip them for the service demanded by the changing aeon.

A + C: Reality and Change Renewing the Pillars

The death of a Pillar makes the world stagger until a replacement is found. The Changing of the Aeons therefore requires succession as well as invention. The mystery of Atrox Fabulinus’s continuing work sharpens the question of who can take up the burden of sustaining the world.

B + C: Being and Change Extraordinary Service

The Group of Twelve is chosen for "extraordinary and onerous service." Their capacities matter through what they undertake: Gorgonius and Denis accept work as Scribbling Giants, while Monika uses her wish to seek replacement of the missing Pillar.

Core Conflict

The Group of Twelve, including a fish tycoon, a piano-maker who has lived in three centuries, and a golden panther, must determine whether their reality is genuine or dream, tested by the hundred-and-one trials of a dead Scribbling Giant whose work mysteriously continues, during the "Changing of the Aeons."

Serpent's Egg

The Magic Dozen and Inneall's Ocean in the Floating World

The novel is set in the "Global Village" of 2035, a "Floating World" governed by the nearly invisible Kangaroo, an elitist interspecies authority that orders assassinations for the common good. Through hybrid killers called the Dolophonoi, it eliminates dangerous "Serpent's Egg" experiments, those that succeed "only too well."

The novel centers on the Lynn-Randal Experiment, which raised together three "super-mega-persons": Lord Randal, an "enhanced" human boy with an off-the-scale IQ; Axel, a gargoyle-faced Axel's Ape whose father founded an underground ape society; and Inneall, an Ambulatory Mime-Human Computer who insists she is the little girl "Bloody Mary Muldoon the Pirate Queen." Inneall created a vast, ever-expanding Ocean.

They are later merged with children from three other experiments to form the Magic Dozen: Marino (a young male seal), Luas (a young male angel), Henryetta (a fiery human girl), Lutin (a prophetic pythoness), Dubu (a young female bear), Schimp (a young male chimpanzee), Gajah (an unborn "Wonder of the World Elephant"), Carcajou (a wolverine of "evil intelligence"), and Popugai (a young male parrot). Guided by the wealthy surrogate grandfather Satrap Saint Ledger, the Dozen gather at Heart's Desire Cove on the shore of their Ocean, aware that as they near their tenth birthdays, the Kangaroo may judge them a Serpent's Egg. The story builds toward "The Three Days of Summerset," a time of foreboding.

Themes

Identity and hybridity Control versus autonomy Evolution of intelligence and species succession Perception and reality The nature of experimentation Fate and prophecy Artifice versus nature Memory, history, and narratives Morality and ethics

A-B-C Diagnostic

A: Reality Floating World and Inneall's Ocean

Reality is a "Floating World": "The Floating World was a giant boat or ark"; any heavier-than-water boat can sink if it ships enough water. Inneall created a vast, ever-expanding Ocean, "extents of water that are standing where no water stood yesterday." In its depths is "the Castle named Domdaniel which is at the bottom of the Ocean."

B: Being Magic Dozen

Being is hybrid and experimental: Lord Randal (enhanced human), Axel (Axel's Ape), Inneall (Ambulatory Computer who made the Ocean), Marino (seal), Luas (angel), Lutin (pythoness), Dubu (bear), Schimp (chimpanzee), Gajah (unborn elephant), Carcajou (evil wolverine). "All of the underwater creatures live in an active state of churning unconsciousness"; "All of them live in dreamworlds."

C: Change Three Days of Summerset

Change comes as threat: the Kangaroo eliminates "Serpent's Egg" experiments through the Dolophonoi. As the Dozen approach their tenth birthdays, they may be judged dangerous. The Ocean Metastasis is "a dream state of incomparable vividness," "the Oceanic Hyper-Active Dream State." The novel invokes the day "When the Sea Shall Give Up Its Dead."

A + B: Reality and Being Experimental Lives in the Floating World

The Magic Dozen come from four experiments that bring different forms of life and intelligence together. Inneall’s ocean then provides a new environment for their activity. The Ocean Metastasis allows them to enter its depths in a vivid dream state, extending what these experimental beings can experience.

A + C: Reality and Change Inneall's Ever-Expanding Ocean

Inneall created an Ocean that keeps expanding, swallowing roads and valleys. Her experiment alters the world, making water stand where none stood before. Such powers expose the children to the Kangaroo’s judgment that an experiment may succeed "only too well."

B + C: Being and Change The Dozen at Heart's Desire Cove

The twelve hybrid children gather at the shore of their own Ocean as their tenth birthdays approach. The Kangaroo may judge them a Serpent's Egg, which could hatch into a new world or be eliminated first by the Dolophonoi. The story builds toward the Three Days of Summerset.

Core Conflict

In the "Floating World" of 2035, the Magic Dozen, children from four experiments, face judgment from the Kangaroo authority as a "Serpent's Egg" that succeeds "only too well," while gathering at Heart's Desire Cove on the shore of Inneall's ever-expanding Ocean for "The Three Days of Summerset."

Cross-Novel Synthesis

Themes shared across the oceanic novels

The Domdaniel Thread

Domdaniel locates hidden powers beneath the sea. In Camels it houses an archetypal company; in East of Laughter it is the meeting place of the Ocean’s governors; in Fair Hills it belongs to an underwater political order. The castle conceals different kinds of authority: archetypal, economic, and political.

Aurelia East of Laughter Serpent's Egg Easterwine Camels Fair Hills

The Polluted Pool

In Sweeny, Widdershins describes collective consciousness as an oil-slick surface above depths haunted by the wars. Camels makes the shared ocean a medium open to purchased influence, while Sandaliotis places a national unconscious in submarine caves. Their depths hold different forms of concealed pressure: historical catastrophe, manipulation, and collective fear.

Sweeny Camels Sandaliotis

The Narrative Sea

Narrative enters these worlds in distinct ways. Space Chantey gives a voyage epic form; East of Laughter and Iron Tongue make the writing of scenarios part of the world’s operation; Sweeny sets rival histories against one another. Klepsis makes access to history a problem. An ocean voyage can carry characters through an epic, into a mind, or toward the powers that write their world.

Klepsis Space Chantey East of Laughter Sweeny Iron Tongue

Crossings and Their Limits

Camels makes Prime World the feared end of world-jumping and evasion of judgment. Sandaliotis tests the apparent solidity of a country; Sindbad carries its quest among worlds and into hidden depths; Fair Hills opens a submerged civilization to land visitors. Pilgrim seeks passage out of judgment; the Fair Hills visitors enter the depths to discover who governs their world.

Camels Sandaliotis Sindbad Fair Hills

The Schizo-Gash

Divided being takes different forms: rival psychic powers in Fourth Mansions, programmed control in Past Master, alien belonging in The Reefs of Earth, and composite personhood in Easterwine. The recurring question is how a person can act with integrity under these different pressures.

Fourth Mansions Past Master Reefs of Earth Easterwine

The Hatching

Eggs, cocoons, and hidden depths give transformation the form of emergence. Serpent’s Egg places experimental lives under threat before maturity; The Elliptical Grave confines its investigators in a pavilion that is also grave, ark, and cocoon. Fourth Mansions makes mutation a contested passage, while Fair Hills brings the submerged world into contact with the surface.
